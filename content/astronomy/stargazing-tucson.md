Tucson is one of the easiest cities in the country to go stargazing from. It has had outdoor lighting rules since the 1970s to protect the observatories around it, so the sky gets dark quickly once you leave town. These are the places I use myself and take groups to for [star parties](/astronomy/starparties/tucson/), from closest to darkest.

## The short list

| Spot | Drive from central Tucson | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Gabe Zimmerman Trailhead | About 35 minutes southeast | A quick trip on a weeknight |
| Saguaro National Park | About 30 minutes east or west | Stars over saguaros without leaving town |
| Ironwood Forest National Monument | About an hour northwest | Wide horizons and a dark western sky |
| Mount Lemmon | About an hour up the Catalina Highway | Cool summer nights at 8,000 feet |
| Oracle State Park | About an hour north | A certified dark sky close to the city |
| Chiricahua National Park | About two hours southeast | The darkest sky in the region |

Drive times are rough and depend on where in town you start.

## Close to town

### Gabe Zimmerman Trailhead

On the Arizona Trail near Vail, southeast of town. It is a quick drive with easy parking, and the sky is a real step up from the city. Tucson's glow stays to the northwest, so the southern and eastern sky are the good part. This is the one I suggest most often to people who want a darker sky without giving up the whole evening.

### Saguaro National Park

The park sits on both edges of the city and was certified as an Urban Night Sky Place in 2023. It is not truly dark, since Tucson is right there, but the Moon, the planets and the bright constellations look great over the saguaros. Park roads and some trailheads close in the evening, so check the park's current hours before you go, and look at its calendar for ranger-led night programs.

### Ironwood Forest National Monument

Open desert northwest of Tucson, past Marana. The horizons are wide, and the western sky is dark because there is very little out that way. The roads are dirt, so go in daylight the first time and bring more water than you think you need.

## Worth the drive

### Mount Lemmon

The Catalina Highway climbs from the desert to more than 8,000 feet in about an hour. Tucson's lights are bright to the south, but the air is thin, cool and steady, and in summer it runs 20 to 30 degrees cooler than the city. The pullouts along the highway are popular for watching the city lights and the stars together. I have hosted a stargazing series for guests at [Mount Lemmon Lodge](https://mountlemmonlodge.com/) in Summerhaven.

### Oracle State Park

A certified International Dark Sky Park since 2014, in the foothills north of the Catalinas. This is where the Milky Way starts to look like its photographs. The park itself is open during the day only. For stargazing, the park offers after-hours access to one of its trailhead lots by reservation, and it runs its own public star parties, so call the park or check its website before you drive up.

### Chiricahua National Park

About two hours southeast, near Willcox. It is America's newest national park and a certified International Dark Sky Park, with some of the darkest skies in Arizona above a landscape of stone spires. On a moonless night the Milky Way runs from horizon to horizon. It is far enough that most people stay the night, in the park campground or in Willcox.

## If you would rather join a public program

- **Mount Lemmon SkyCenter** runs evening programs with large telescopes near the summit.
- **Kitt Peak National Observatory**, southwest of Tucson, offers night programs by reservation.
- **Oracle State Park** and **Saguaro National Park** both hold public star parties and night walks through the year.
- I set up telescopes on University Boulevard for free whenever I can. See [sidewalk astronomy](/astronomy/sidewalk-astronomy/).

## Getting the most out of a night

- **Check the Moon first.** A bright Moon washes out the Milky Way and faint objects. For dark-sky viewing, go within about a week of new Moon. The Moon itself is best in a telescope around first quarter.
- **Give your eyes time.** It takes 20 to 30 minutes to adapt to the dark, and one look at a white phone screen undoes it. Use a red light.
- **Know the season.** The bright summer Milky Way is in the evening sky from about June through October. Winter has Orion, the Pleiades and the brightest stars of the year. Spring is galaxy season.
- **Watch the monsoon.** From late June through early September, afternoon storms often leave the evening cloudy, and it is not fully dark until after 9 pm.
- **Dress for it.** The desert cools fast after sunset, even in summer. Bring more layers than you think you need.
- **Be ready for the desert.** Closed-toe shoes, water, a full tank, and a look at the map before you lose cell coverage.
