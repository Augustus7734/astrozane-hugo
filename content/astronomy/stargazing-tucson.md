Tucson is one of the easiest cities in the country to go stargazing from. It has had outdoor lighting rules since the 1970s to protect the observatories around it, so the sky gets dark quickly once you leave town. These are the places I use myself and take groups to for [star parties](/astronomy/starparties/tucson/), from closest to farthest.

Drive times are rough and depend on where in town you start.

## Do you need to call ahead?

Mostly no. Trailheads, national forest land and other ordinary public land need no reservation and no phone call. You just go.

National parks and state parks are the exception. They have gates, hours and their own rules, so call or check ahead before you drive out. On this list that means Saguaro National Park, Chiricahua National Park and Oracle State Park.

## In town or right next to it

### Sabino Canyon parking lot

On the northeast side, at the foot of the Catalinas. It is not that dark, since the city is right behind you, but it is the most convenient place on this list. It works well for the Moon, the planets and the bright constellations.

### Tanque Verde lower trailhead

Out Redington Road on the far east side. It is a bit darker than Sabino and nearly as easy to get to.

### Saguaro National Park

The park sits on both edges of the city and was certified as an Urban Night Sky Place in 2023. It is not truly dark, since Tucson is right there, but the Moon, the planets and the bright constellations look great over the saguaros. Park roads and some trailheads close in the evening, so check the park's current hours before you go, and look at its calendar for ranger-led night programs.

### Gabe Zimmerman Trailhead

On the Arizona Trail near Vail, southeast of town. It is a quick drive with easy parking, and the sky is a real step up from the city. Tucson's glow stays to the northwest, so the southern and eastern sky are the good part. This is the one I suggest most often to people who want a darker sky without giving up the whole evening.

### Ridgeline Loop trailhead

A mountain bike trailhead in the Tortolita foothills on the north side. It is an easy option if you are staying in Oro Valley or Marana.

## About an hour out

### Ironwood Forest National Monument

Open desert northwest of Tucson, past Marana, about 45 minutes from town. The horizons are wide, and the western sky is dark because there is very little out that way. The roads are dirt, so go in daylight the first time and bring more water than you think you need.

### Empire Ranch

In the Las Cienegas grasslands north of Sonoita. Rolling open grassland with low horizons in every direction and very little light nearby.

### Whipple Observatory astronomy pads

On Mount Hopkins Road near Amado, about an hour south. The observatory built a small area for amateur astronomers, with concrete pads and benches at about 5,000 feet. It is one of the few places around Tucson made for setting up a telescope.

### Apache Springs Trailhead

An Arizona Trail trailhead on Gardner Canyon Road, north of Sonoita in the foothills of the Santa Ritas. The last five and a half miles are dirt. There is a big parking area and a dark sky.

### Mount Lemmon

The Catalina Highway climbs from the desert to more than 8,000 feet in about an hour. Tucson's lights are bright to the south, but the air is thin, cool and steady, and in summer it runs 20 to 30 degrees cooler than the city. The pullouts along the highway are popular for watching the city lights and the stars together. I have hosted a stargazing series for guests at [Mount Lemmon Lodge](https://mountlemmonlodge.com/) in Summerhaven.

### Oracle State Park

A certified International Dark Sky Park since 2014, in the foothills north of the Catalinas. This is where the Milky Way starts to look like its photographs. The park itself is open during the day only. For stargazing, the park offers after-hours access to one of its trailhead lots by reservation, and it runs its own public star parties, so call the park before you drive up.

## Worth a longer drive

### Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge

Southwest of Tucson, toward Arivaca and Sasabe. The sky here is almost perfect. There is very little development for miles, and the grassland gives you a full horizon.

### Aravaipa Canyon west trailhead

About 90 minutes north, past Mammoth. It is remote, very dark and very quiet. The trailhead parking area is all you need; hiking into the canyon itself takes a permit.

### Chiricahua National Park

About two hours southeast, near Willcox. It is America's newest national park and a certified International Dark Sky Park, with some of the darkest skies in Arizona above a landscape of stone spires. On a moonless night the Milky Way runs from horizon to horizon. It is far enough that most people stay the night, in the park campground or in Willcox.

### Mount Graham, Columbine Corrals

The campground and parking area at Columbine, near the top of the Swift Trail in the Pinaleño Mountains, sit at about 9,400 feet. It is about three hours from Tucson, so plan to camp. The upper road is closed from mid-November to mid-April.

## If you would rather join a public program

- **Mount Lemmon SkyCenter** runs evening programs with large telescopes near the summit.
- **Kitt Peak National Observatory**, southwest of Tucson, offers night programs by reservation.
- **Oracle State Park** and **Saguaro National Park** both hold public star parties and night walks through the year.
- I set up telescopes on University Boulevard for free whenever I can. See [sidewalk astronomy](/astronomy/sidewalk-astronomy/).

## Getting the most out of a night

- **Check the Moon first.** A bright Moon washes out the Milky Way and faint objects. For dark-sky viewing, go within about a week of new Moon. The Moon itself is best in a telescope around first quarter.
- **Give your eyes time.** It takes 20 to 30 minutes to adapt to the dark, and one look at a white phone screen undoes it. Use a red light.
- **Know the season.** The bright summer Milky Way is in the evening sky from about June through October. Winter has Orion, the Pleiades and the brightest stars of the year. Spring is galaxy season.
- **Watch the monsoon.** From late June through early September, afternoon storms often leave the evening cloudy, and it is not fully dark until after 9 pm.
- **Dress for it.** The desert cools fast after sunset, even in summer. Bring more layers than you think you need.
- **Be ready for the desert.** Closed-toe shoes, water, a full tank, and a look at the map before you lose cell coverage.
