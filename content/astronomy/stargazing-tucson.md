Tucson is one of the easiest cities in the country to go stargazing from. It has had outdoor lighting rules since the 1970s to protect the observatories around it, so the sky gets dark quickly once you leave town. These are the places I use myself, roughly from closest to farthest.

**In the Phoenix area instead?** See my guide to [stargazing spots near Phoenix and Scottsdale](/astronomy/stargazing-near-phoenix/).

## Do you need to call ahead?

Mostly no. Trailheads, national forest land and other ordinary public land need no reservation and no phone call. You just go.

National Park Service sites and state parks are the exception. They have gates, hours and their own rules, so call or check ahead before you drive out. On this list that means Saguaro National Park, Chiricahua National Park, Coronado National Memorial (Montezuma Pass), Oracle State Park, Kartchner Caverns State Park and Patagonia Lake State Park.

## In town or right next to it

### Sabino Canyon parking lot

On the northeast side, at the foot of the Catalinas. It is not that dark, since the city is right behind you, but it is convenient. It works well for the Moon, the planets and the bright constellations.

### Saguaro National Park

The park sits on both edges of the city and was certified as an Urban Night Sky Place in 2023. The drive and the sky are basically the same as Sabino: not truly dark, but the Moon, the planets and the bright constellations look great over the saguaros. Park roads and some trailheads close in the evening, so check the park's current hours before you go.

### Tanque Verde lower trailhead

Out Redington Road on the far east side. It is a bit darker than Sabino and nearly as easy to get to.

### Gabe Zimmerman Trailhead

On the Arizona Trail near Vail, right by I-10 southeast of town. It is a quick drive with easy parking, and the sky is a real step up from the city. Tucson's glow stays to the northwest, so the southern and eastern sky are the good part. This is the one I suggest most often to people who want a darker sky without giving up the whole evening.

### Ridgeline Loop trailhead

A mountain bike trailhead in the Tortolita foothills on the north side. It is an easy option if you are staying in Oro Valley or Marana.

## Mount Lemmon

The Catalina Highway climbs from the desert to more than 9,000 feet. Tucson's lights are bright to the south, but the air is thin, cool and steady, and in summer it runs 20 to 30 degrees cooler than the city. There are three places worth knowing.

### Windy Point Vista

The shortest drive of the three, about 45 minutes from town, with a gorgeous view. It is actually farther from most of the light domes than the top of the mountain is. The air here is very steady on average. It is the only place where I can regularly use more than 500x on the planets.

### Summerhaven

The village near the top, at about 8,000 feet. It is not as high as the summit, but it is also less windy. [Mount Lemmon Lodge](https://mountlemmonlodge.com/), where I have hosted a stargazing series for guests, is here.

### Mount Lemmon summit

The highest of the three, above 9,000 feet. It has the best transparency, very good seeing, and no oncoming headlights to ruin your night vision.

## About an hour out

### Ironwood Forest National Monument

Open desert northwest of Tucson, past Marana, about 45 minutes from town. The western sky and most of the south are essentially pristine. The roads are dirt, so go in daylight the first time and bring more water than you think you need.

### Empire Ranch

In the Las Cienegas grasslands north of Sonoita. The sky is very good for the drive time, and there is a lot of wide-open space with low horizons in every direction. The historic ranch house is a museum, which makes it worth arriving before sunset.

### Whipple Observatory astronomy pads

On Mount Hopkins Road near Amado, about an hour south. The observatory built a small area for amateur astronomers, with concrete pads and benches at about 5,000 feet. It is one of the few places around Tucson made for setting up a telescope.

### Kartchner Caverns State Park

South of Benson, about an hour from Tucson, and a certified International Dark Sky Park. There is a campground, so it works well as an overnight. It is a state park, so check hours and fees ahead.

{{< zl-img src="/astronomy/stargazing/kartchner-caverns-state-park-telescope-stargazing.jpg" alt="An observer at the eyepiece of a 6-inch Newtonian telescope under the stars at Kartchner Caverns State Park, Arizona" caption="Observing with my 6-inch at Kartchner Caverns State Park." width="560" >}}

### Apache Springs Trailhead

An Arizona Trail trailhead on Gardner Canyon Road, north of Sonoita in the foothills of the Santa Ritas. The last five and a half miles are dirt. There is a big parking area and a dark sky.

### Oracle State Park

A certified International Dark Sky Park since 2014, in the foothills north of the Catalinas. Honestly, the sky is not much better than Empire Ranch, Ironwood Forest or the Whipple pads, and those need no reservation. The park itself is open during the day only. For stargazing it offers after-hours access to one of its trailhead lots by reservation, for a $12 fee, so call the park before you drive up. Go in knowing what to expect: there are often imagers there with lights on, cars come and go on the road, and there is a near-constant hum from a natural gas turbine across the street.

## Worth a longer drive

### Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge

Southwest of Tucson, toward Arivaca and Sasabe. The sky is essentially pristine apart from skyglow low in the north, from about north-northwest to north-northeast. That glow comes from Phoenix and Casa Grande, not Tucson. The grassland gives you a full horizon.

### Patagonia Lake State Park

South of Sonoita, near Patagonia. A dark sky with a campground, so it is an easy overnight. It is a state park, so check hours and fees ahead.

### Aravaipa Canyon west trailhead

About 90 minutes north, past Mammoth. It is remote and very quiet, and the sky is essentially pristine apart from light pollution low in the west, from about west-southwest to west-northwest. That comes as much from the Phoenix area and the prisons at Florence as it does from Tucson. The trailhead parking area is all you need; hiking into the canyon itself takes a permit.

### Cochise Stronghold

In the Dragoon Mountains, southeast of Tucson. A dark sky among granite domes, with a campground.

### Parker Canyon Lake

Southeast of Sonoita, close to the border. It is remote and dark, with camping at the lake.

### Montezuma Pass

In Coronado National Memorial, south of Sierra Vista, at about 6,500 feet. The view south runs deep into Mexico. The lights along the border are very dim and do not affect stargazing. The memorial is a National Park Service site, so check its hours before you go.

## The Chiricahuas and Mount Graham

### Chiricahua National Park

About two hours southeast, near Willcox. It is America's newest national park and a certified International Dark Sky Park, with some of the darkest skies in Arizona above a landscape of stone spires. The two places to set up are the Echo Canyon trailhead and Massai Point. It is far enough that most people stay the night, in the park campground or in Willcox.

### Onion Saddle and Rustler Park

In the national forest above the park. Onion Saddle is even higher and darker than the national park, but the dirt road up to it is rough. Rustler Park, a little farther along, is also very good, though trees block part of the sky. These are for a trip on your own. I do not take star party groups up there.

### Mount Graham, Columbine Corrals

The campground and parking area at Columbine, near the top of the Swift Trail in the Pinaleño Mountains, sit at about 9,400 feet. It is about three hours from Tucson, so plan to camp. The upper road is closed from mid-November to mid-April.

## Others worth knowing about

- **Cascabel.** There are a lot of good dark spots along the San Pedro River around Cascabel, north of Benson. I do not have one specific pullout to send you to.
- **San Manuel.** I used to set up on a dirt road next to the old San Manuel copper mine, which is farther from Tucson's glow than Oracle is. New mining activity there has spoiled it for now.
- **Willow Springs Road.** The area north of Oracle Junction where the 24 Hours in the Old Pueblo mountain bike race is held. I have not observed from there myself, but it should be good. Check land access before you go.

## Getting the most out of a night

- **Check the Moon first.** A bright Moon washes out the Milky Way and faint objects. For dark-sky viewing, go within about a week of new Moon. The Moon itself is best in a telescope around first quarter.
- **Give your eyes time.** It takes 20 to 30 minutes to adapt to the dark, and one look at a white phone screen undoes it. Use a red light.
- **Know the season.** The bright summer Milky Way is in the evening sky from about June through October. Winter has Orion, the Pleiades and the brightest stars of the year. Spring is galaxy season.
- **Watch the monsoon.** From late June through early September, afternoon storms often leave the evening cloudy, and it is not fully dark until after 9 pm.
- **Dress for it.** The desert cools fast after sunset, even in summer, and the mountain sites are cold most of the year. Bring more layers than you think you need.
- **Be ready for the desert.** Closed-toe shoes, water, a full tank, and a look at the map before you lose cell coverage.
